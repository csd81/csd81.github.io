**2.1. Review of Calculus**

## 1. Basic Notations and Function Sets

The notes distinguish between function sets according to the smoothness (degree of differentiability) of functions by introducing the following sets:

* **$C[a,b]$:** The set of real-valued, **continuous** functions defined on the closed interval $[a,b]$.
* **$C^m[a,b]$:** The set of functions that are continuous on $[a,b]$ and **$m$ times continuously differentiable** on the open interval $(a,b)$.

### Spanned Open Form of the Interval ($\langle \cdot \rangle$)

The open interval generated by real numbers $a$ and $b$ (where their order or magnitude is not necessarily fixed) is denoted as follows:

$$\langle a,b\rangle := (\min\{a,b\}, \max\{a,b\})$$

As a generalization, $\langle a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n \rangle$ denotes the smallest open interval spanned by the minimum and maximum of the listed numbers.

## 2. Extreme Value Theorem and Intermediate Value Theorem

### Weierstrass Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 2.1)

> If $f \in C[a,b]$, then the function **attains its maximum and minimum** on the closed interval $[a,b]$. That is, there exist $c, d \in [a,b]$ such that:
> 
> $$f(c)=\max_{x\in[a,b]} f(x) \qquad \text{and} \qquad f(d)=\min_{x\in[a,b]} f(x)$$

### Rolle's Theorem (Theorem 2.2)

If $f \in C[a,b]$ and is differentiable on the open interval ($f \in C^1[a,b]$), and its values at the endpoints of the interval are equal ($f(a)=f(b)$), then there exists at least one interior point $\xi \in (a,b)$ where the function's **tangent is horizontal**, i.e., its derivative is zero:

$$f'(\xi) = 0$$

### Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem (Theorem 2.3)

A generalization of Rolle's theorem for the case when the endpoint values differ. If $f \in C^1[a,b]$, then there exists an interior point $\xi \in (a,b)$ where the slope of the tangent is equal to the slope of the secant line connecting the endpoints:

$$f'(\xi) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \implies f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)(b-a)$$

## 3. Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem and Taylor's Theorem

### Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem (Theorem 2.4)

If $f, g \in C[a,b]$ and are differentiable on the open interval, and $g'(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \in (a,b)$, then there exists a point $\xi \in (a,b)$ such that:

$$\frac{f'( \xi )}{g'( \xi )} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$$

### Taylor's Theorem (Theorem 2.5)

Taylor's theorem allows the approximation of a sufficiently many times differentiable function by a polynomial in the neighborhood of a point $x_0$, while providing an exact formula for the error of the approximation (remainder term).

If $f \in C^{n+1}[a,b]$ and $x_0 \in (a,b)$, then for every $x \in [a,b]$ there exists an interior point $\xi \in \langle x, x_0 \rangle$ such that the function can be written as the sum of the $n$-th degree Taylor polynomial and the Lagrange remainder term:

$$f(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2}(x - x_0)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n + \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x - x_0)^{n+1}$$

## 4. Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals and Cantor's Intersection Theorem

### Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals (Theorem 2.6)

> Let $f \in C[a,b]$ be a continuous function, and $g\colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be an integrable function that **does not change sign** on the interval $[a,b]$ (i.e., $g(x) \ge 0$ or $g(x) \le 0$ throughout). Then there exists a number $\xi \in (a,b)$ such that:
> 
> $$\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx = f(\xi)\int_a^b g(x)\,dx$$

### Cantor's Intersection Theorem (Theorem 2.7)

This theorem ensures the theoretical convergence of numerical interval-narrowing procedures (such as the bisection method).

> Let $[a_n, b_n]$ be a sequence of bounded and closed intervals for which it holds that they are nested in each step, i.e., $[a_{n+1}, b_{n+1}] \subset [a_n, b_n]$ for all $n$. If the length of the intervals tends to zero at infinity, i.e., $(b_n - a_n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, then:
> **There exists exactly one common point $c \in \mathbb{R}$** which is an element of each closed interval ($c \in [a_n, b_n]$ for all $n$).
